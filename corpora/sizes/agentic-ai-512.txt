## Executive summary

Three years after tool-calling language models became reliable enough to put in
production, the agentic layer of the software industry has settled into a shape
that would have been hard to predict in 2024. The dominant architecture is not a
society of autonomous agents negotiating with one another. It is a single capable
model in a tight loop with a small set of well-described tools, a carefully
engineered context window, and a human somewhere on the escalation path. Almost
every successful deployment looks like that loop wrapped in ordinary software
engineering: retries, idempotency keys, audit logs, and a kill switch.

The capability story of 2026 has been less about raw model scale and more about
inference-time compute, context length, and the quality of the scaffolding. Models
that can hold two hundred thousand tokens of working state and spend a variable
amount of thinking per step have made long-horizon tasks tractable: multi-file
refactors, multi-day research syntheses, claims adjudication, migration work. The
hard problems that remain are unglamorous. Evaluation is still mostly bespoke.
Prompt injection remains unsolved in the general case and is now the primary
security concern for any agent with both private data access and untrusted input.
Cost discipline, not capability, decides most build-versus-buy arguments.

The most consequential shift for individual practitioners has been the collapse in
the cost of competent local inference. Open-weight models in the four-to-forty
billion parameter range, quantised to four bits and served by commodity runtimes,
now handle summarisation, extraction, classification, routing and much light
