## Executive summary

Three years after tool-calling language models became reliable enough to put in
production, the agentic layer of the software industry has settled into a shape
that would have been hard to predict in 2024. The dominant architecture is not a
society of autonomous agents negotiating with one another. It is a single capable
model in a tight loop with a small set of well-described tools, a carefully
engineered context window, and a human somewhere on the escalation path. Almost
every successful deployment looks like that loop wrapped in ordinary software
engineering: retries, idempotency keys, audit logs, and a kill switch.

The capability story of 2026 has been less about raw model scale and more about
inference-time compute, context length, and the quality of the scaffolding. Models
that can hold two hundred thousand tokens of working state and spend a variable
amount of thinking per step have made long-horizon tasks tractable: multi-file
refactors, multi-day research syntheses, claims adjudication, migration work. The
hard problems that remain are unglamorous. Evaluation is still mostly bespoke.
Prompt injection remains unsolved in the general case and is now the primary
security concern for any agent with both private data access and untrusted input.
Cost discipline, not capability, decides most build-versus-buy arguments.

The most consequential shift for individual practitioners has been the collapse in
the cost of competent local inference. Open-weight models in the four-to-forty
billion parameter range, quantised to four bits and served by commodity runtimes,
now handle summarisation, extraction, classification, routing and much light
coding at a quality that was frontier-grade eighteen months earlier. Mixture-of-
experts architectures with small active parameter counts changed the hardware
calculus: a thirty-five billion parameter model with three billion active
parameters decodes at conversational speed on a mid-range consumer GPU, provided
the weights fit in memory. What local deployment buys is privacy, determinism and
marginal cost near zero. What it costs is prefill throughput at long context,
which is exactly where consumer hardware is weakest and where measurement matters
most.

## 1. What "agentic" came to mean

The word was contested for two years and has since narrowed to something useful. An
agentic system is one in which a model decides what to do next, repeatedly, against
an environment it can change — as opposed to a pipeline in which a human or a fixed
program decides the sequence of calls. The distinction is about control flow
ownership, not about autonomy, intelligence, or how impressive the demo looks.

Practitioners now talk about four rough tiers. **Assistive** systems propose and a
human commits every change; most enterprise rollouts start here and many
deliberately stay. **Supervised agentic** systems execute multi-step plans but
check in at defined gates — a pull request, a draft invoice, a proposed refund.
**Bounded autonomous** systems run to completion inside a sandbox with a budget
and a blast radius, and are judged on outcomes rather than steps; coding agents
operating on a branch are the canonical example. **Open autonomous** systems, with
unbounded tool access and no review, remain rare outside research, and the reasons
are mostly liability and security rather than capability.

The tiering matters because it predicts where engineering effort goes. Assistive
systems live or die on latency and interface design. Supervised systems live or die
on the quality of the gate: what gets surfaced to the reviewer, and how much
context the reviewer needs. Bounded autonomous systems live or die on
verification — whether the system can tell, without a human, that the work is
actually done.

## 2. The loop that won

The architecture that generalised is embarrassingly simple. A model receives a
goal, a set of tool schemas, and whatever context has been assembled. It emits
either a tool call or a final answer. Tool results are appended and the loop runs
again. Everything interesting is in the details: which tools exist, how they are
described, what goes into the context, and when the loop is allowed to stop.

Two design principles have hardened into consensus. The first is that tools should
be few, orthogonal and coarse-grained. Twenty overlapping micro-tools produce
worse behaviour than five well-chosen ones, because selection error compounds over
a long trajectory and every schema consumes context. The second is that tool
descriptions are prompts and deserve the same scrutiny: naming, argument shape,
error strings and examples all measurably change behaviour. Teams that version and
test their tool descriptions outperform teams that treat them as documentation.

The loop's failure modes are well catalogued. Models get stuck repeating a failing
call with minor variations; they declare success without verifying; they lose track
of the original goal after a long detour; they silently narrow scope to the part of
the task they can do. Mitigations are correspondingly mundane: step budgets,
duplicate-call detection, an explicit verification step before finishing, and
periodic re-grounding of the goal in the context window.

## 3. Context engineering

Context engineering replaced prompt engineering as the central craft. The window is
a budget, and the question is always what deserves space in it. Four techniques
account for most of the practice.

**Compaction** replaces old turns with a summary when the window fills. Done
naively it destroys exactly the detail the agent will need later; done well it is
lossy in a controlled way, preserving decisions, open questions and file paths
while discarding tool output bodies. Most mature stacks compact asymmetrically,
keeping the first instruction block and the most recent turns verbatim.

**Retrieval** pulls in only what the current step needs. The 2026 consensus is that
retrieval for agents looks less like classical RAG and more like giving the model
good search tools and letting it navigate — grep and read over embed and hope,
especially over codebases and structured corpora. Embedding retrieval persists
where the corpus is large, unstructured and semantically indexed.

**Memory tiers** separate the scratchpad from durable facts. Working memory is the
window; episodic memory is the trajectory log; semantic memory is a curated store
of learned facts and preferences. The hard part is write policy, not read policy:
deciding what is worth remembering, and garbage-collecting what has gone stale.
Systems that write too eagerly poison themselves within weeks.

**Sub-agents** are a context management device as much as a division of labour. A
sub-agent explores a large surface — reads forty files, runs a dozen searches — and
returns a conclusion, so the parent's window absorbs the answer rather than the
search. This is the one multi-agent pattern that is unambiguously load-bearing in
production.

## 4. Tool interfaces and protocols

Tool integration standardised faster than most observers expected. A client-server
protocol for exposing tools, resources and prompts to models became the common
substrate, which collapsed the N-by-M integration problem: a tool server written
once is usable by any compliant host. The practical effect is an ecosystem of
reusable servers for filesystems, databases, browsers, ticketing systems and
internal APIs, and a corresponding supply-chain question about what a given server
is actually allowed to do.

Agent-to-agent interoperability progressed more slowly and remains mostly
aspirational outside controlled settings. Cross-organisation delegation runs into
identity, authorisation, auditability and payment problems that are institutional
rather than technical, and the economic case is thin when a direct API call works.

Computer use — agents driving a GUI by screenshot and synthetic input — matured
into a reliable fallback rather than a primary interface. It is slow, expensive in
tokens, and brittle against interface change, but it is the only option for systems
with no API, and it carries a distinctive risk profile because the agent's actions
are indistinguishable from a user's in most audit trails.

Sandboxing is now table stakes. The standard pattern is a container or
microVM per session, with an explicit egress allowlist, a read-only mount of
anything the agent must not modify, and credentials scoped to the narrowest role
that permits the task. The sandbox is the primary defence against both model error
and injection, because it is the only layer that does not depend on the model
behaving well.

## 5. Capability and inference-time compute

The scaling story of 2026 is that quality became purchasable at inference time.
Models trained to use a variable thinking budget turned latency into a tuning
parameter: the same model answers a routing question in fifty tokens and a
proof-shaped question in fifteen thousand. For agentic work this is more valuable
than it sounds, because long-horizon tasks are dominated by a handful of hard
decisions surrounded by easy mechanical steps, and spending uniformly on every step
is wasteful.

The practical corollary is that cost modelling got harder. A single agent run no
longer has a predictable token count; it has a distribution with a long tail. Teams
that budget on medians get surprised monthly. The mitigations are per-run token
caps, thinking-budget ceilings per step class, and routing: a small model triages,
a large model handles the few steps that need it. Routing is where local inference
earns its place in otherwise cloud-based stacks.

Context length grew but the useful length grew more slowly. Models with very large
nominal windows still degrade on retrieval in the middle of a long context, and
attention cost means that filling a window is rarely the cheapest way to solve a
problem. The operational rule of thumb is that a long window is a safety margin
rather than a design target, and that engineering the context down is almost always
cheaper than paying to ingest it.

## 6. Evaluation

Evaluation is the least solved part of the stack and the most reliable predictor of
whether a team ships something that works. Public agentic benchmarks — software
engineering issue resolution, web navigation, tool-use suites, long-horizon
research tasks — are useful for model selection and nearly useless for product
decisions, because the distribution of tasks in any real deployment is narrow,
idiosyncratic and drifting.

What works in practice is a small, curated, versioned task set drawn from real
traffic, graded at the trajectory level rather than the final-answer level. Did the
agent take an irreversible action it should have escalated? Did it verify before
declaring done? Did it spend forty steps on something that needed four? These
process questions predict production behaviour better than answer accuracy, and
they are also what regression testing needs: a change that preserves accuracy while
doubling step count is a regression.

Model-graded evaluation is now standard and is understood to be biased in specific
ways: graders favour verbose answers, reward surface structure, and are unreliable
on precisely the hard cases where a human would disagree with another human. The
discipline that makes it work is calibration — a periodically re-graded human gold
set, and tracking grader-human agreement as a first-class metric.

Benchmark contamination and benchmark gaming both got worse as agentic evaluations
became commercially salient. The credible response has been private held-out sets,
rolling task refreshes, and reporting cost and step count alongside accuracy, since
an agent that solves more tasks by spending ten times more compute is a different
product, not a better one.

## 7. Reliability engineering

Agentic systems fail in ways that distributed systems engineers find familiar and
application developers do not. The failure taxonomy that teams converged on
separates **capability failures** (the model cannot do the task), **grounding
failures** (it acts on a wrong belief about the environment), **specification
failures** (it does what was asked rather than what was meant), and
**compounding failures** (an early small error makes every later step wrong).
Only the first is addressed by a better model.

The engineering response is to treat every tool call as a potentially retried,
potentially duplicated operation. Idempotency keys on anything that mutates state;
checkpointing so a failed run resumes rather than restarts; explicit
compensating actions for partial completion; and a transactional boundary around
sequences that must not half-apply. None of this is novel. It is simply that the
caller is now nondeterministic, which removes the usual excuse for skipping it.

Observability for agents needs trajectory-level primitives. A span per tool call is
necessary but insufficient; what operators actually need is the ability to replay a
run with its exact context, diff two runs of the same task, and search trajectories
by behaviour ("find runs where the agent retried the same call more than three
times"). Teams that built this early debug in minutes; teams that did not debug by
reading transcripts.

## 8. Security

Prompt injection is the defining unsolved problem. Any system that mixes
instruction-bearing untrusted content with private data access and the ability to
act externally is exploitable in principle, and no amount of prompt hardening
changes that, because the model has no reliable channel-level distinction between
instruction and data. The practical framing that stuck is that the danger requires
the conjunction of untrusted input, sensitive data and an exfiltration path, and
that the engineering job is to break the conjunction rather than to make the model
immune.

The defences that work are architectural. Keep untrusted content out of the
privileged context entirely, processing it in a quarantined agent whose output is
structured data rather than prose. Enforce egress allowlists so there is no channel
to exfiltrate to. Require human confirmation for actions that are irreversible or
externally visible. Scope credentials per task, not per application. Treat tool
output as hostile input and validate it against a schema before it reaches the
model.

The confused-deputy problem is the organisational mirror of this. An agent acting
with a service account's permissions will happily do, on behalf of a low-privilege
requester, anything that account can do. The fix is to propagate the requester's
identity into the tool layer and authorise there, which most internal APIs were not
built to support and which accounts for a surprising share of agent platform
engineering effort.

Supply chain risk arrived with the tool-server ecosystem. A tool server is code
running with the agent's privileges, and its description is an instruction the model
will follow, so a malicious or compromised server is both a code-execution and a
prompt-injection vector. Pinning versions, reviewing descriptions as code, and
running third-party servers in their own sandbox are the emerging hygiene.

## 9. Economics

Three cost curves shape the market. Per-token prices for a given capability level
have fallen steeply and continue to; prompt caching made repeated-prefix workloads
dramatically cheaper and reshaped how agent contexts are structured, since a stable
prefix is now worth engineering for. And inference-time compute pushed in the
opposite direction, letting teams spend arbitrarily more per task for quality.

The consequence is a barbell. Cheap, small, fast models absorb the enormous volume
of mechanical steps — classification, extraction, routing, summarisation, patch
generation for trivial diffs. Expensive, slow, high-compute models handle the
decisions that actually need judgement. The middle is squeezed. Stacks designed
around a single model for everything are usually paying two to five times more than
necessary, or shipping lower quality than they could.

Local inference is the extreme end of the cheap pole. Marginal cost is electricity;
fixed cost is a GPU; privacy is structural rather than contractual. The economics
work when volume is high and the task is one a small model does well, and they fail
when the workload is bursty, needs frontier capability, or needs long-context
prefill on hardware that cannot do it quickly. Measuring that last constraint on
specific hardware is the only way to know which side of the line a workload falls
on.

## 10. Local and open-weight agents

The open-weight ecosystem in 2026 covers the low and middle of the capability range
competently. Four classes of local deployment are common: small dense models in the
one-to-nine billion parameter range for embedded and high-volume work; mid-size
dense models around twenty to thirty billion for general assistant quality;
sparse mixture-of-experts models with large total and small active parameter counts
for throughput; and quantised large models run slowly for privacy-critical batch
work.

Quantisation is the enabling technology and the main source of confusion.
Four-bit weight quantisation of a well-trained model typically costs a few percent
on knowledge benchmarks and rather more on tasks needing precise arithmetic or long
chains of reasoning, and the loss is uneven across models. The practical guidance is
that a larger model quantised harder usually beats a smaller model quantised
lightly, until roughly three bits, below which degradation becomes erratic.

The hardware constraint that matters for agentic work is not parameter count but
the key-value cache. Cache size grows linearly with context length and with the
number of attention key-value heads, which modern architectures reduce
aggressively through grouped-query and multi-head latent attention. A model whose
weights occupy six of eight available gigabytes has two gigabytes for cache, which
at long context is the binding constraint. When the cache or the weights do not
fit, runtimes offload to system memory, and throughput falls by an order of
magnitude or more because every token must cross the bus.

This produces the characteristic performance shape of consumer-hardware agents.
Decode throughput is memory-bandwidth-bound and roughly constant with context
until offload begins, at which point it collapses. Prefill is
compute-bound and scales with available arithmetic throughput, so it is where
newer architectures with tensor cores and faster memory show their largest
advantage over older cards — often several-fold, far more than the gap in nominal
memory bandwidth would suggest. Energy per token follows the same pattern, and
energy is the metric that translates most directly into whether a local deployment
makes sense at volume.

For agentic workloads specifically, prefill dominates. An agent re-ingests a
growing context on every step, so a loop with twenty steps over a twenty-thousand
token context pays prefill twenty times unless prefix caching is working. This is
why prefill throughput at realistic context lengths, rather than decode speed on a
short prompt, is the number that predicts whether a local model is usable as an
agent — and why measuring it per card, per model, per context length is worth the
trouble.

## 11. Orchestration stacks

The platform layer consolidated into a recognisable set of components: a model
gateway handling routing, fallback, rate limits and spend caps; a tool registry
with versioning and permissions; a session store for trajectories and memory; a
sandbox provisioner; an evaluation harness; and an observability plane. Teams
either buy this as a managed platform or assemble it, and the assembly is
substantial but not research-grade work.

Framework fatigue was real and has largely resolved in favour of thin libraries
over opinionated frameworks. The loop itself is thirty lines of code; what teams
actually need is the surrounding infrastructure, and heavyweight abstractions over
the loop tended to obstruct exactly the context control that determines quality.
The surviving frameworks are the ones that stayed close to the model API and
invested in the platform concerns instead.

Durable execution emerged as the most useful borrowed idea. Modelling an agent run
as a workflow with persisted state, replayable steps and timers solves resumption,
long-running human approval gates, and the operational need to migrate a running
agent across deployments. The pattern imposes discipline on side effects that
agentic code otherwise lacks.

## 12. Multi-agent systems

The evidence for multi-agent architectures is narrower than the discourse. Three
patterns earn their complexity. **Sub-agent delegation** for context isolation, as
described above. **Parallel fan-out** over independent work items, where a dozen
agents each handle one file or one document and a cheap aggregation step combines
results. **Adversarial review**, where a second agent with a different prompt and
no stake in the first agent's conclusion checks the work — reliably better than
asking the same agent to self-critique.

Patterns that mostly do not earn their complexity include role-playing teams of
specialists with elaborate personas, free-form negotiation between peers, and
hierarchies deeper than two levels. The failure mode is consistent: coordination
overhead and error propagation grow faster than the division of labour helps, and
debugging becomes extremely hard because no single trajectory explains the
outcome.

The honest summary is that multi-agent systems are an optimisation for context
limits and parallelism, not a route to emergent capability. A single strong model
with good tools and well-managed context is the baseline to beat, and it is a
higher baseline than most multi-agent papers assume.

## 13. Enterprise adoption

Adoption settled into a predictable progression. Organisations start with
assistive deployments in a single function, usually support, sales engineering or
internal documentation search. The second phase is a supervised agentic workflow
with a review gate, where the measurable win is reviewer throughput. The third
phase, which many have not reached, is bounded autonomy on a narrow,
well-instrumented process with a clear verification signal.
